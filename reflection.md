# Reflection

**With 3 months instead of 8 hours**, the first change would be persistent, approved knowledge: an alias/mapping table with approver identity, so a renamed account is resolved by a human once and is deterministic forever after — right now every run starts ignorant. Second, I'd build the tie-out suite before any statement logic, especially the completeness check (sum of statement lines equals sum of mapped TB balances), since that's the one that catches an account mapping nowhere while every other check still passes. Third, lineage would be written at computation time, not reconstructed afterward — a reconstructed trail is wrong in exactly the way the computation was wrong. What I would *not* change is the deterministic core and the refusal to let a model touch numbers; more time makes that boundary easier to defend, not harder.

**Where it breaks at scale**, in order: memory (in-memory dicts are fine at ~80 accounts, not at thousands across dozens of entities — needs a relational store with append-only ledger tables); the account key (keying on account code alone doesn't survive consolidation, which needs `(entity, account, currency)` — this propagates through every index and control, so it's the structural decision I'd revisit first); concurrency (multiple reviewers approving mappings against one open period need period locks, or they silently overwrite each other); and elimination as a global graph pass rather than per-entity, with its own tie-outs pre- and post-elimination. There's also a quieter failure: at 4,000 accounts across 30 entities, an unclustered exception queue is functionally the same as no output.

**How I used AI tools.** They helped with fast scaffolding of the mechanical ingestion/indexing layer, enumerating adversarial failure cases I hadn't listed (header-account posting, sign-convention inconsistency), and pressure-testing the topology argument by having a model argue the other side. They led me wrong in three ways. First, a pull toward multi-agent over-architecture — the default suggestion was autonomous Mapper/Adjuster/Builder/Validator agents, which is wrong here because execution order is fixed and known in advance, so autonomy buys nothing. Second, plausible-but-wrong accounting detail — suggestions on FX treatment of equity and cash-flow classification were fluent and not reliably correct, the dangerous kind of error since it doesn't look wrong on inspection; these became open questions rather than implemented behavior. Third, a bias toward cleaning the data — the reflexive suggestion for a duplicate code or unbalanced TB was to deduplicate or plug the difference, exactly wrong here since a dropped row becomes the system's defect, not the source's. Net: strongly positive on breadth and speed, needed close supervision on architectural restraint and domain correctness.

**What I think you're underestimating:** mapping approval is a governance problem, not a modelling one, and it's where this product succeeds or fails. Once a mapping decides which statement line a balance lands on, *who may approve it* is an audit control — it needs segregation of duties between preparer and approver, and immutability once a period closes. Confidence scores should change queue ordering, not approval authority; auto-applying above a threshold quietly moves an accounting judgment from a named person to a model, which an auditor will reject regardless of accuracy. The hard version of this problem isn't "get the mapping right" — it's "prove, eighteen months later, who got it right and why." That's a provenance requirement that has to be designed on day one; it can't be retrofitted.
